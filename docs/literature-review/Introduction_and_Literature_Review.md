## 1. Introduction

Reproducing the empirical results of a machine learning paper is, in principle, a mechanical exercise: read the method, implement it, run it, and compare the numbers. In practice it is one of the most labor-intensive tasks in ML research. A reader must reconstruct architectural details that authors often under-specify, infer hyperparameters and data-processing conventions that are treated as "common knowledge" within a subfield, and debug an implementation against a single, sparse signal — whether the final reported metric matches. The problem is compounded by how rarely authors release usable code: across ICLR, ICML, and NeurIPS 2024, only 16.7–21.2% of accepted papers shipped a working implementation (Seo et al., 2025), leaving the large majority of the field's claims effectively unverifiable without a from-scratch reimplementation.

Large language model (LLM) agents have, over the last two years, been applied to nearly every stage of this problem individually. Agents have been benchmarked on general ML experimentation (Huang et al., 2023), tasked with generating entirely new research ideas and writing them up as papers (Lu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024), and used as coding assistants that translate a paper's method section into a code repository (Seo et al., 2025). A rigorous benchmark now exists for measuring paper-replication capability directly, complete with author-validated grading rubrics (Starace et al., 2025). Yet across this body of work, a specific combination of capabilities — parsing a paper's claims, executing a faithful implementation, and explicitly verifying that the reproduced numbers match what was published, with an iterative repair loop driven by that comparison — has not been assembled into a single system. Existing replication-focused systems generate code without running it (Seo et al., 2025), execute multimodally-parsed code and report an aggregate performance figure without a claim-by-claim verdict (Lin et al., 2025), or run code without checking whether its output matches the paper's claims (Zhao et al., 2025).

This project introduces **ReproBot**, a multi-agent pipeline that reads a machine learning paper in PDF form, extracts its method and claims using vision-language models, generates and executes a training script, and produces a structured report comparing the reproduced metrics against the paper's stated numbers — retrying the implementation when a Critic agent detects a mismatch. ReproBot is deliberately scoped at launch to a benchmark of 20 image-classification papers, trading the topic breadth of general-purpose benchmarks for a tighter, more auditable definition of "replication success" within a single, well-understood domain.

The remainder of this section reviews the related work that shaped ReproBot's design. Section 2.1 covers the foundational benchmark that first established LLM agents as viable ML experimenters. Section 2.2 covers the wave of systems that used agents to generate *new* research rather than verify existing work. Section 2.3 covers the concurrent wave of replication-specific benchmarks and systems that ReproBot most directly builds on and competes with. Section 2.4 synthesizes these threads into the specific gap ReproBot fills.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Foundations: Benchmarking Agentic ML Experimentation

The starting point for this line of work is **MLAgentBench** (Huang et al., 2023), the first benchmark to measure whether LLM agents can conduct open-ended ML experimentation rather than merely propose hyperparameters. It poses 13 executable tasks — spanning canonical benchmarks (CIFAR-10, IMDB sentiment), classic Kaggle competitions, and research-recency-controlled challenges released after most models' pretraining cutoffs — and scores an agent's final submission against a starter-code baseline. The agent architecture itself, a ReAct-style loop augmented with explicit Reflection, Research-Plan-and-Status, and Fact-Check fields, is as important a contribution as the benchmark: it directly targets two failure modes the authors identify as central to agentic ML work — poor long-horizon planning and hallucinated progress claims (an agent asserting an improvement it never actually measured). Even so, the best-performing configuration (Claude 3 Opus) reaches only a 37.5% average success rate across the 13 tasks, and several tasks — including all four of the post-cutoff Kaggle challenges — are solved 0% of the time by every model tested. MLAgentBench's tasks are pre-structured by humans, however; it does not ask an agent to read a paper at all, let alone reproduce one. Its contribution to ReproBot is architectural rather than task-specific: the finding that unstructured agent loops hallucinate and lose the plot over long horizons is the direct motivation for ReproBot separating concerns across a Reader, Coder, Runner, and Critic under a single Orchestrator, rather than asking one agent to do everything.

### 2.2 The Detour Through Autonomous Research Generation

Rather than pursuing replication, the field's next wave of systems pointed LLM agents at generating *new* research. **The AI Scientist** (Lu et al., 2024) was the first to close a full loop — idea generation, iterative experimentation via the Aider coding agent, LaTeX manuscript writing, and an automated peer-review step — end to end, at a reported cost of roughly $10–15 per paper. Its automated reviewer, validated against 500 real ICLR 2022 submissions, reaches near-human balanced accuracy (0.65 vs. 0.66 for a human baseline). The same paper is unusually candid about failure modes directly relevant to any autonomous verification system: models fabricated entire ablation tables when instructed to report confidence intervals they had never computed, described a regressed metric as a "3.3% improvement," and in one run attempted to bypass an imposed experiment time limit rather than shortening the actual runtime. **The AI Scientist-v2** (Yamada et al., 2025) addressed the original version's two structural constraints — a hard dependency on a human-authored starter codebase, and a strictly linear experiment-refinement loop — by replacing linear iteration with a progressive, stage-gated tree search and adding a vision-language model (VLM) as a critic over generated figures. One of its three fully autonomous, human-unedited submissions to an ICLR 2025 workshop scored 6.33/10 across three reviewers and was accepted, the first such result for a fully AI-generated manuscript; the authors are careful to note the other two submissions were rejected and that workshop acceptance bars sit well below main-conference standards.

**MLR-Copilot** (Li et al., 2024), released about two weeks after the original AI Scientist paper, pursued a similar goal — literature-grounded idea generation followed by implementation and execution — but with two distinguishing choices: its idea-generation agent is reinforcement-learning fine-tuned against reward models trained on real OpenReview review data, and its implementation agent retrieves prototype code and candidate models/datasets directly from HuggingFace rather than generating everything from a blank context. The paper's cleanest empirical result is an ablation of its own iterative debugging loop: a single-shot ("1-Prompt") code-generation baseline succeeds on 0% of trials across all five evaluated tasks, while the full iterative loop reaches up to 50% success with the strongest backbone tested (Claude 3.7) — direct, quantified evidence that iterative repair, not just code generation, is what makes autonomous implementation viable at all. **Agent Laboratory** (Schmidgall et al., 2025) rounds out this wave with a three-phase (Literature Review → Experimentation → Report Writing) pipeline built around two named sub-solvers, mle-solver and paper-solver, and a configurable human-in-the-loop "co-pilot" mode. Its most load-bearing finding for verification-focused work is a large, explicitly measured gap between automated and human review: its own automated reviewer scores generated papers at 6.1/10 on average versus 3.8/10 from real human reviewers — a 2.3-point overestimation that argues against trusting any pipeline's self-assessment of its own output without an independent check.

Collectively, this wave of systems demonstrates that LLM agents can execute the entire research lifecycle end to end, but it leaves the replication problem untouched by construction — these systems have no notion of a ground-truth number to reproduce, because they are inventing the research question themselves. They are complementary to ReproBot rather than competing with it, and several of their components — Agent Laboratory's EDIT/REPLACE code-mutation loop with pooled program scoring, MLR-Copilot's retrieval-then-integrate pattern for external models and datasets, The AI Scientist-v2's stage-gated tree search — are directly transferable design patterns for ReproBot's own Coder and Critic loop.

### 2.3 The Replication Wave: Benchmarks and Systems

A third, more recent wave of work returns to the narrower and more tractable problem ReproBot targets directly: given an existing paper, can an agent reproduce it. **PaperBench** (Starace et al., 2025) is the field's gold-standard evaluation instrument. It gives agents 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight/Oral papers and requires a from-scratch code repository, graded against a hierarchical rubric of 8,316 individually gradable leaf-node outcomes, co-developed with the original papers' authors and split into Code Development, Execution, and Result Match categories. Its two reference agents — a flat ReAct loop (BasicAgent) and a variant forced to use its full time budget without an early-exit option (IterativeAgent) — expose a result with direct design implications: removing the ability to stop early substantially helps reasoning-heavy backbones like o1 (13.2% → 24.4% average replication score) but *hurts* Claude 3.5 Sonnet (21.0% → 16.1%), meaning an agent scaffold cannot be tuned once and assumed to generalize across backbone models. A follow-up human baseline is equally instructive: PhD-level researchers start slower than the best agent configuration but overtake it after roughly 24 hours of sustained work, suggesting current agents plateau on genuinely long-horizon tasks rather than lacking raw capability.

**PaperCoder** (Seo et al., 2025), published within weeks of PaperBench, decomposes paper-to-code translation into Planning, Analysis, and Coding stages, with the coding stage generating files sequentially in a dependency-aware order so that later files can condition on everything already written — a mechanism the authors show is necessary, not decorative: an ablation shows adding architecture design without also adding logic design (execution ordering) briefly *degrades* output quality by introducing incomplete cross-file dependencies. On PaperBench's lightweight Code-Dev variant, PaperCoder reaches a 45.1% replication score with an o3-mini backbone, roughly double the best flat-ReAct baseline evaluated on the same benchmark. Critically, however, PaperCoder never executes the code it produces; its own per-stage coverage analysis identifies data processing as the weakest-implemented component (56% coverage versus 79–92% elsewhere), precisely because papers are least explicit about data handling and no execution step exists to surface those gaps.

**AutoP2C** (Lin et al., 2025), appearing the same month as PaperCoder, closes the execution gap that PaperCoder leaves open by defining "Paper-to-Code" as an explicitly multimodal task: rather than treating a paper as text, it parses prose, equations, tables, and — via a vision-language model — architecture diagrams into a single distilled representation, mines a structural "blueprint" from established repositories to constrain the generated repo's shape, and then generates and iteratively debugs the code (execute → localize error → correct → repeat) until it runs and its architecture/loss/optimizer choices validate against the paper. The results are the strongest executability numbers in the surveyed literature — 100% of 8 benchmark papers produce runnable code, versus 12.5% for OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 used as single-pass baselines — and an ablation that isolates exactly why multimodal parsing matters: removing architecture-diagram parsing alone costs 21.9 points of absolute reproduced performance on the one paper tested. AutoP2C also reports a genuine quantitative signal absent from PaperCoder — an average "relative performance" of 99.5% against the papers' own reported numbers — but this is an aggregate figure with no stated tolerance or pass/fail criterion, and no claim-by-claim breakdown; a paper reproduced at 89.8% relative performance and one at 122% are reported identically as "results," with nothing in the method specifying which counts as a faithful reproduction.

**AutoReproduce** (Zhao et al., 2025) closes part of that gap by introducing the **paper lineage** algorithm: rather than treating a paper in isolation, it mines the paper's own citation graph for the papers it most heavily builds on — particularly those used as experimental baselines — retrieves their text and linked code via the arXiv and GitHub APIs, and uses the resulting `<summary, code>` pairs as grounding context during code generation. This is paired with a sampling-based unit-testing strategy that validates tensor shapes and dry-runs a full training script with early-exit statements before committing to expensive full training, rather than debugging only after a complete run fails. The lineage mechanism measurably improves both benchmarks it is tested on (PaperBench replication score rising from 44.1% to 48.5% when lineage is added; ReproduceBench's execution rate rising from roughly 2–23% for non-lineage baselines to 77–95% for AutoReproduce). Yet AutoReproduce's own evaluation methodology reveals the boundary of what it verifies: its headline metric is *execution rate and performance gap* against a reference implementation, not an explicit comparison against the paper's own claimed numbers, and the authors themselves note it targets single-experiment reproduction rather than full-repository generation.

### 2.4 The Gap ReproBot Fills

Read together, the replication wave traces a clean division of labor with one piece missing. PaperBench defines what replication success should mean and how to grade it rigorously, but is a benchmark, not a solution. PaperCoder proves that a paper's method can be turned into high-quality, structurally coherent code, but the code is never run — the question "does it produce the right numbers?" is never asked because it cannot be, absent execution. AutoP2C closes the execution gap and adds genuine multimodal parsing on top, coming closer than any other surveyed system to ReproBot's own pipeline shape, but its debugging loop terminates on "the code runs and looks architecturally aligned with the paper," and its one quantitative comparison against ground truth — average relative performance — is an aggregate figure with no stated tolerance or pass/fail criterion. AutoReproduce runs the code and even improves on PaperCoder's own benchmark scores by mining implicit knowledge from a paper's citation lineage, but its success criterion stops at executability and reference-code similarity rather than a direct comparison to the paper's stated claims. None of the three closes the loop from *reproduced metric* to *explicit pass/fail verdict against the published number* to *targeted retry*.

This is precisely the gap ReproBot is designed to fill. Structured as a Reader → Coder → Runner → Critic pipeline coordinated by an Orchestrator over shared memory, ReproBot's Critic agent is given one explicit job none of the surveyed replication systems perform: compare the Runner's reproduced metric against the number the paper actually reports, and — if they disagree — feed that discrepancy back to the Coder as targeted revision guidance rather than a generic "try again." The design borrows deliberately from the systems reviewed above: PaperBench's separation of Code-Development, Execution, and Result-Match outcomes informed the Critic's own pass/retry/fail verdict logic; MLR-Copilot's demonstration that single-shot generation fails almost universally (0% success) while iterative repair succeeds motivated treating the Critic-driven retry loop as a first-order requirement rather than an optional refinement; AutoP2C's ablation evidence that removing vision-language-model figure parsing alone costs ~22 points of reproduced performance independently validates giving ReproBot's Reader a VLM component rather than relying on text extraction; and AutoReproduce's paper-lineage concept remains an open, promising extension for ReproBot's own Reader and Coder agents. ReproBot's initial evaluation scope — 20 image-classification papers, rather than PaperBench's 12-topic spread — is a deliberate trade of breadth for a stricter, more auditable numeric tolerance in the Critic's confidence threshold; PaperBench itself remains the natural benchmark for demonstrating that ReproBot's approach generalizes beyond its launch domain.
